    Riley County Had A “Bad” Year, According To Dr. Evans

    There Were Four Deaths From Contagious Diseases During 1937-Many Mumps Cases Were Reported

    Riley Country had a “bad” year so far as diseases are concerned, according to a report by Dr. D.L. Evans, county health officer.

    Dr. Evans reported a big increase in the number of contagious diseases for Riley county during 1937. He said there were four deaths from contagious diseases during the year. These included one from typhoid fever, one from whooping cough, and two from infantile paralysis.

    How Diseases Were Divided

    The diseases were divided as follows for the past two years: Disease19361937Scarlet Fever56 151Mumps67361 Measles2018Chicken Pox18257Whooping Cough4381Diptheria15Small Pox010Pneumonia4927Typhoid Fever01Infantile Paralysis03Miscellaneous2422

    Asked what he believed was repsonsible for the big increase in the number of diseases during the past year, Dr. Evans pointed out that there were a large number of cases of mumps and chicken pox this year in comparison to last year and that these cases boosted the total to a high figure.

    Quick Travel Is A Factor

    Discussing causes for the increase, Dr. Evans said there were several things which may have been responsible. He said that at the present time transportation of human beings from one place to another is fast and germs may be spread from one vicinity to another in a quick manner by swift travel.

    He said the economic factor must be considered. There are times, he explained, when residents do not have money to spend on health care and thus refrain from seeing their physician when becoming ill. In many cases the individual does not report the illness to the physician immediately and thus the germs are spread and more persons are affected.

    Dr. Evans also termed weather as a factor in the increase of disease.

    Breathing Easy In “Lung”

    The city's “iron lung,” purchased two and a half years ago from funds raised by popular subscription, is in perfect condition and ready to help save the life of any…

    Read it, with the original

    The city's “iron lung,” purchased two and a half years ago from funds raised by popular subscription, is in perfect condition and ready to help save the life of any victim of infantile paralysis, as this reporter, invited to try the device can testify.

    At the order of the health department, members of the city police and fire departments dusted the respirator off Saturday and checked every part to see that it functioned properly and then tested the “lung” on three “patients,” Undersheriff Frank Toburen, Police Chief Arthur Scheleen and this reporter.

    One after the other, the “patients” were placed on the cylindrical tank's cushioned bed, head protureuding through the sponge rubber mat at one end and resting on a cushioned shelf. At the turn of a switch, a shaft from the machine's motor began slowly to move a large diaphragm which made up the tank's foot end.

    As the diaphragm moved in and out of the “lung,” it alternately compressed and relieved the air inside and the “patient” soon found it much more conventient to “breathe with the lung.” When the rubber mat was permitted to rest rightly against his throat thus making the tank almost air tight, the tester was able to relax and let the “lung” do his breathing for him.

    At the end of a few minutes in the tanks, this reporter decided he had slept on beds infinitely less comfortable than the “lung's” and had to do his own breathing.

    “Polio” Closes Local Schools

    Dr. W.M. Reitzel Announces Public And Parochial Classes Will Not Be Resumed Monday

    Read it, with the original

    Dr. W.M. Reitzel Announces Public And Parochial Classes Will Not Be Resumed Monday

    Manhattan public and parochial schools will not open Monday morning as scheduled but will reamin closed “until further notice,” it was announced Saturday night by Dr. W.M. Reitzel, county health officer.

    In ordering that city schools be kept closed, Dr. Reitzel said he was acting on instructions from the state board of health, which directed the move in order to combat futher spread of infantile paralysis.

    Rural schools in Riley county, most of which opened last Monday, will continue to operate-with the exception of the Hunter's Island Alert and Walnut Creek schools. Dr. Reitzel closed the latter school of six pupils Saturday pointing out that it was near the Alert school district in which one case of poliomylelitis has broken out.

    There has neen no announcement concerning colleges and univrersities in the state since the board said Friday tha tthey would open on schedule.

    It is expected the state board of health will have a futher recomendation after its meeting in Topeka Monday morning, when it will consider latest developments of the disease which is having its most virulent epidemic in the state since 2930.

    Co-operate Gladly

    Dr. W.E. Sheffer, city superintendent, said Saturday night that the school board would co-operate wholeheartedly with the health authorities and Msgr. A.J. Luckey indicated the Sacred Heart academy also would remain closed.

    Dr. Sheffer issued the following statement:

    “Throughout the week, Dr. Reitzel has been in contact with health officials in Topeka, and I have been kept informed by him. In view of the situation, it seemed advisable to keep the school closed, but in order to make doubly sure Dr. Reitzel Saturday called state epidemiologist Dr. C.H. Kinnaman, who advised the schools be closed.”

    No More Cases Here

    “Naturally the board of education is anxious to do the right thing. It very readily accepts the judgement and instruction of the constituted authorities and therefore co-operates without question.”

    Meanwhile, Dr. Reitzel reported no further cases of infantile paralysis had broken out in the county. Three cases of the disease, two of which still are active have been reported this summer.

    The last serious outbreak of infantile paralysis in the state occurred in 1930, when there were 694 cases and 68 deaths. Many of the others were left cripples. About 90 percent of the sufferers usually are children under 15, but this year persons in the 15 to 30 age group have been stricken.

    During most of July and August of this year, the number of cases of the disease ran ahead of the corresponding weeks in 1930. The peak 10 years ago was September with 272 cases, and officials are hoping that preventive measures taken this year have started a downward trend.

    Urges Clean-up During Epidemic

    County Health Officer Points To Measure In Fighting “Polio”

    Read it, with the original

    County Health Officer Points To Measure In Fighting “Polio”

    “Get Rid Of All Types Of Trash In Back Yards And Alleys,” Dr. W.M. Reitzel Recommends To Citizens

    Dr. W.M. Reitzel, county health officer, today urged citizens to clean their yards as a health measure in fighting the current epidemic of infantile paralysis.

    He recommended the cleaning up of all types of debris and rubbish and not to wait until the regular clean-up week.

    “Get rid of all kinds of trash in the back yards and allets,” the health officer advised. “This may eliminate the possible spread of ‘polio’.”

    He said rubbish which would attract rats and mice and other rodents especially should be cleared away. “Follow all rules of sanitation,” he said, “in an effort to keep down the disease.”

    Weeds Should Be Cut

    He urged also the cutting of weeds and recommended that where there is filth, lime should be spread over the area.

    Dr. Reitzel approved the plans of the city in paving allets in Manhattan. At the present time a resolution is pending for paving of the alley between the First Christian church and the library. A petition now is out asking for the alley between Sixteenth and Seventeenth streets and between Poyntz and Humboldt to be paved.

    According to the city engineer construction costs now would be as low as they ever would be.

    Advantages To Paving

    If the alleys are paved, it was pointed out, citizens would be more apt to pile tin cans and other trash in proper containers. When the alleys are paved there is more desire on the part of the citizen to keep alleys clean, thereby reducing the disease hazard.

    Dr. Reitzel said that from the health standpoint the city should look to more perfect drainage of alleys to and streets. He said he hoped the plan for better drainage of Bluemont streey would be carried out to eliminate low places on the street. Dr. Reitzel said these low places are breeding spots for mosquitoes and bacteria.

    However, Coach Prentup And School Officials Plan To Play All Other Games As They Have Been Scheduled

    The “polio” epidemix struck in new territory this morning with the announcement that the first game on Manhattan high school's fall football schedule - with Lawrence here Friday night - has been cancelled definitely and will not be played at a later date.

    Because of the polio epidemic, which closed all schools in Manhattan last week, health officials decided that the interests of safety could be served by also calling off football practices for the local high school team. So Coach Frank Prentup's Indians have been doing nothing but resting since a week ago today, when the last practice was held.

    Nearly all the other schools in the state have been going ahead with their practice sessions and the locals may get back in the swing any day now that the epidemic seems to be taking a downward trend. However, Coach Prentup feels that the boys would not be in good enough shape physically to play the game Friday if only two or three days of workouts are avalable before the game.

    “Just because we have been foreced to cancel this first game, don't think the high school won't have a football season,” Prentup said this morning. “We plan to meet every game on schedule throughout the reaminder of the season.”

    The game with Lawrence was to have been a non-league affair as Manhattan is playing in the new Central Kansas Conference this year. Both the local school and Lawrence were formerly members of the old Eastern Kansas Conference, which disbanded after the football season last fall. The next game on the schedule is a league affair with the Junction City Blue Jays, who were conference champs last year.

    Pearce-Keller post of the American Legion and its auxillary will furnish a room in the Riley county hospital, it was nnounced jointly today by Commander Art Garansson and Mrs. Chester Drake, president of the auxillary, Garansson said.

    “Our gift of the furnishings for the room in the hospital is in keeping with the central feature of the American Legion, ‘For God and Country.’ For sometime we have served the community by sponsoring junior league baseball and child welfare activities. We have provided wheel chairs free of charge for any persons needing them. The “iron lung” which we presented to the community a few years ago was damaged beyond repair by the flood of 1951.

    “We consider that the room at the hospital will be a way of giving help to an untold number of persons in this community for years to come. At the same time, it will give us a satisfaction and pride in our organization which cannot be measured in terms of finnacial cost,” the two said in their announcement.

    Town Lived Up To Its Name In Fight Against Polio

    Read it, with the original

    Protection - It has been almost 50 years, but Bonnie Brown still remmbers being inoculated against polio.

    “We all lined up and had our little ol' shots,” she said. “That's the way we did the ... thing. We went to the schoolhouse, and we all lined up like a bunch of cattle.”

    Millions of Americans were vaccinated in the 1950s against polio, which caused paralysis and sometimes death in many of its victims.

    Protection, then a community of about 1,000 residents in south-central Kansas, has a unique distinction in the war against the disease: It was selected to be the first community in the country with 100 percent immunization against the virus - all because of the town's name.

    This year marks the 50th anniversary of the first nationwide tests of the polio vaccine, discovered by Jonas Salk. According to the March of Dimes, 2 million children took part in those tests.

    In 1957, three years after the first tests, 32-year-old Brown and her young children, along with the rest of their friends and neighbors, lined up in the Protection High School gymnasium, rolled up their sleeves and received shots to protect themselves from the disease.

    “It's a litle town, so when we do something, we do it together,” resident Pat Sanders said. “It was a real united effort where children, parents and grandparents took part.”

    Community members were well aware of the disease, which had stricken a handful of the community's residents.

    “The thing that was really frightening was the out oldest son had been at the swimming pool when an olderman was there who died from polio shortly after,” Doris Anne Leeper said. “After that, we were really cautious about letting our kids swim and get overtired because they said that's how it would happen.”

    “Everybody's got a shot. We ran all seven of our kids through, and we did it, too.”

    The Foundation for Infantile Paralysis, now the March of Dimes, chose Protection as the site for 100 percent participation, and used photographs and other literature - much of it postmarked by the Protection Post Office - to advertise the importance of obtaining the vaccine, said Protection resident Dave Webb.

    Using the Protection postmark was a technique also employed by a computer company offering services to protect computers from the “Y2K” scare in 1999.

    The foundation's alliterative slogan, “Everybody in Protection is protected agfainst polio, and you can be too,” spread across the world in news stories, advertisements and motion pictures.

    “Even soldiers from our area serving in Germany ran into the newsreel footage,” Webb said.

    Legend has it it that the name “Protection” came from a fort built in response to an “Indian scare” in the 1880s, but Webb said the name came from the group of Republicans responsible for the first post office in town who supported protective tariffs.

    The Salk vaccine was issued in a series of doses. In Protection, the first shot was distributed on April 2, 1957, and the second on April 24.

    To celebrate, Protection residents held the “Polio Protection Pow-Wow” on the day of their second innoculation.

    Country music star Clyde Julian “Red” Foley flew in by helicopter, and cowboy stuntman Jock Mahoney appeared on horseback. Mary Ann McGrew, Miss Kansas 1956, was crowned “Miss Protection” for the festivities, attended by 4,000 people.

    “I took it all in,” said Florence Riner, now 95. “I was at the age where it sure was fun.”

    Riner remembers the Pow-Wow as a “beautiful” day.

    “I really had a good time,” she said. “My husband was a rodeo man, and we had a big rodeo to celebrate, and a barbecue.”

    Certain events of the day stand out for Riner: the long lines and her youngest daughter square-dancing dring the celebration.

    And the importance of their actions on those days remains.

    “We didn't know it would turn out to be as big a deal as it was,” Riner said. “It doesn't seem like that long ago.”

    The Manhattan Mercury
