    At Fort Riley, Kansas, an Army private reports to the camp hospital just before breakfast on March 11 complaining of fever, sore throat, and headache.   • April 6, 1918Riley County

    Urge Sneezeless Days

    Army Medics Begin Campaign On Respiratory Diseases

    Read it, with the original

    Army Medics Begin Campaign On Respiratory Diseases

    Washington, April 6.-Respiratory diseases having caused practically all the sick and death rate in the nation's new armies, the Medical Corps today began a nation-wide campaign of prevention against pneumonia, measles, diptheria, mumps, meningitis, infantile paralysis, influenza and tuberculosis.

    All of the diseases mentioned are spread or communicted in the same way-through the transfer of nasal and mouth secretions. The sickness rate in camps throughout the country could be cut to the minimum if the soldiers and the general public as well, stopped promiscuous coughing, sneezing and spitting.

    The difficulty of controlling these diseases, it was pointed out at the Surgeon General's office today, lies in the fundamental fact that human beings scatter their nose and mouth secretions without any consideration, despite rules and regulations to the contrary. One sneeze in a crowded street car could infect many persons in the car with pneumonia, or measles, or diptheria, or mumps, or meningitis, or infantile paralysis, or tuberculosis. One hearty cough could do the same. In either case spread of any of these disease could be prevented if a sneezer or a cougher used his handkerchief as a “cough catcher” to sneeze or cough in.

    Unlike some disease germs, the germs which cuase the disease mentioned do not die or become harmless upon drying. So that when they are dried and blown about with the dust they are still capable of setting up disease in many other people. They problem of the respiratory dieases which confronts Surgeon General Gorgas and the Army Medical Corps is serious. Apparently, however, the public generally is totally ignorany of the immeasurable harm that is done through unguarded and promiscuous coughing, sneezing and spitting. Army Medical Corps experts, who have given untiring to this problem, have reached the conclusion thata national campaign must be inaugerated at once, not only in the camps but in the homes, in the offices, in the street cars, and in all public places, to stamp out this widespread habit of spitting and coughing, if health conditions for our soldiers are to be improved.

    They feel that such a campaign is as important in every way for the object to be achieved as was the now famous and eminently successful fly swatting campaign which started some years ago. For this reason the Surgeon General therefore, appeals to the public to make the “Don't cough, don't sneeze, don't spit” slogan as widely quoted and effective as “Swat the fly.” The Surgeon General appeals particularly to the press of the country to help with constant warnings, priminently displayed in their columns, in the form of articles and catch slogans that will serve as reminders to the public, that the health of our soldiers can be better safeguarded through vigilant co-operation in the anti-cough-and-spit campaign.”

    The Manhattan Mercury

  • Oct. 1, 1976Nurse Battey

    The Spanish Lady and the Devoted Nurse

    Sara Hillary Battey was weary and fearful as she walked down thee wooden steps of the three-story, wood-frame house at 511 North Juliette and past the double row of elms…

    Read it, with the original

    Sara Hillary Battey was weary and fearful as she walked down thee wooden steps of the three-story, wood-frame house at 511 North Juliette and past the double row of elms on the avenue. It was early in the week of October, 13, 1918, and she was on her way to the community house where she was helping care for cadets in the student army training corps who lay ill with influenza. She had been reading pleas in the Manhattan Nationalist that volunteers were needed to help make gauze masks. The 28-year-old Red Cross worker had been living in an apartment at the home of George Adam Dean, an assistant professor of entomology, and her husband, Capt. Percy B. Battey, was in the medical corps and was stationed at nearby Camp Funston.

    Because the United States was deeply involved in World War I, student cadets were being trained at the college. New barracks were mobved into at the start of the fall term in September.

    A hint of disaster began on September 16 when there were 47 influenza cases reported at Camp Funston. That figure swelled to 15,170 by November 1.

    Meanwhile, the Young Men's Christian Association (YMCA) became a temporary hospital in Manhattan as did the Sigma Alpha Epsilon and Beta Theta Pi fraternity houses. As the disease spread, cadets in the 1,120-member corps were quarentined. They were told to remain on the campus or in the fraternity houses. If they did not, violations would consist of two month's kitchen police duty.

    Students were told not to go home unless they could travel by some other means than the railroad. Cadets started reporting for sick call in the doctor's office on the first floor of the barracks. The aggie students began going to the college physician's office in room 65 of Anderson Hall. Both of the swimming pools in Nichols Gym were closed and the campus drinking fountains were declared not safe. The inaugeration of Dr. William M. Jardine as president was postponed.

    Friday, October, 11, by a proclamation from Kanas Governor Arthur Capper, the school was closed and remained closed until November 11. (It was clsoed a second time from December 9 to 30.)

    In the meantime, Sara Battey was among hundreds of volunteers who calmly went about work while others became frantic as the epidemic worsened. At one time Manhattan clothing merchants ran out of pajamas so they went to Wamego where they bought 100 pairs. Women in town got busy and made 8,265 face masks (they were soaked in a disinfectant); each mask took about 30 minutes to make. Telephone operators went through the phone book alphabetically and called residents until they had volunteered 100 pillows. There also was a call for bedsocks; barbershops allowed chair-capacity only (one had to wait outside the shop); merchants gave each customer only 10 square feet of space; and fruits and jellies were needed by the sick.

    Worldwide-from October through February-more than 20 million lives were lost. Not since the Black Death of the Middle Ages, which took a higher toll, had such a tragedy occurred.

    It is believed that in May of 1918 an early outbreak in San Sebastian, Spain, was hushed up by local authorities because they were afraid tourist trade might suffer. By the end of the month, though, some eight million Spaniards were stricken.

    There were four students, all men from Kansas, who died along with seven cadet Nebraska soldiers who were on the campus but were not students. The students were:

    There were some faculty: William Henry Ball who taught woodworking and who died of pneumonia following influenza as did B.O. Severson, an authority on animal breeding, who had just come to the colelge from Pennsylvania.

    The epidemic was not prevalent among the women students. Only three cases were reported.

    The college had had earlier scares. There was an epidemic of measles during 1886 and 1887 with 42 cases and two deaths. And nearly one-half of students in the early months of 1890 had the flu and related diseases but the school was not closed.

    All told, more than 12,000 Kansans died...
