Introduction

The 1918 influenza pandemic was the most severe pandemic in recent history. Arriving during the final months of World War I, the H1N1 virus of avian origin killed more people in twelve weeks than the war had in four years.

Although there is not universal consensus regarding where the virus originated, it created a worldwide crisis from 1918 to 1919.

1918 Facts

  • As the pandemic swept the world, the average life expectancy in the United States dropped by 12 years.
  • By November 1, 1918, Camp Funston at Fort Riley had reported 15,170 cases of influenza. The fall wave began there on September 16, with 47 cases reported that day.
  • In 1918 there were no flu vaccines, no antiviral drugs and no antibiotics to treat the pneumonia that followed. Influenza vaccines were not developed until the 1940s.

Get the Anti-Flu Habit

A vast crowd fills a city street under flags and confetti at the first news of peace, November 11, 1918.

Spring 1918

Influenza was a known disease in 1918, but it was traditionally a mild illness with symptoms departing after a few days. Seasonal outbreaks occurred annually with generally minor effects. Just as in prior years, in the spring of 1918 the flu swept through many U.S. Army training camps.

As a result of this seasonal outbreak, Camp Funston at Fort Riley, near Manhattan, Kansas, eventually became the target of rumors. These rumors led some researchers to place the spring outbreak as the beginning of the much more catastrophic one that came in the fall.

However, despite a higher than average number of cases being reported, few of the patients infected in the spring succumbed to the illness. This has led some researchers, such as Jed Dunham, to declare the spring event an entirely independent bout of seasonal flu, unrelated to the fall pandemic which took millions of lives. Clearing this misconception would also clear Camp Funston as the starting point of the infection.

In Riley County

  • On March 11, 1918, Albert Gitchell, a cook at Fort Riley, reported to sick call with a cold. He is often named as the first recorded case of the 1918 flu.
  • On April 6, 1918, the Manhattan Mercury reported that army medics were beginning a nationwide campaign against respiratory diseases, urging soldiers and the public to stop “promiscuous coughing, sneezing and spitting.”

Long rows of beds in an emergency hospital ward at Camp Funston, Fort Riley, Kansas, 1918.

Naming the Illness

The seasonal flu which began in North America in the spring may have been taken to Europe by American soldiers on their way to the war. Once it arrived in France, it spread rapidly.

Largely unrestricted and unaffected by the ongoing war, Spain was the primary country reporting on the spread of the highly contagious seasonal flu in Europe. As in North America that spring, there was a higher than average number of flu cases reported, and a low number of deaths.

As newspapers in the U.S. began to write stories about this illness spreading in Spain and the rest of Europe, they began calling it the “Spanish Flu.” The name became ingrained in the public mind in the following months, as the separate pandemic strain spread in the fall.

Summer 1918

Around August 27, sailors stationed on board the Receiving Ship at Commonwealth Pier in Boston began reporting to sick bay with the usual symptoms of influenza. By August 30, over 60 sailors were sick.

Soon Commonwealth Pier was overwhelmed, and 50 cases had to be transferred to Chelsea Naval Hospital. Flu sufferers commonly described feeling as if they had been beaten all over with a club.

This sickness had most of the common symptoms of the known influenza, but it seemed to bring much stronger reactions from patients, and many ultimately died. It was unlike any flu that had come before.

What Is a Pandemic?

Most people are sickened by influenza by the age of five. If a person survives (most flu deaths traditionally strike the very young or the very old), that person develops immunities which help to combat future arrivals of the seasonal illness.

A pandemic occurs when a new version of the disease evolves for which there are no known immunities. This is what happened in 1918.

The 1918 pandemic struck people aged 25 to 40 the hardest. Modern medical science has yet to definitively answer why, but it seems plausible that the flu this age group was exposed to in childhood (1890 to 1900) was different from the H1N1 influenza which erupted in 1918.

Arriving with a cough or a sneeze, within days it could plunge a community into a nightmare of catastrophic despair.

Prevent Influenza

Public health posters asked people to cover coughs and sneezes, avoid crowds, wash their hands and wear gauze masks in sick rooms.

A line of police officers in long coats and caps wearing white gauze masks, 1918.

The Story of Nurse Battey

Surpassing any possibility of containment, by October the pandemic had reached Manhattan, Kansas. The available resources in the community were rapidly overwhelmed.

Kansas State Agricultural College, today Kansas State University, was heavily affected. One group living on campus, the Student Army Training Corps, was met with the highly contagious pandemic strain. To handle the high number of patients, help was sought from outside the community. A nurse named Sarah Battey answered that request.

At the time, influenza was not recognized as a deadly disease. As people began dying of the new strain in large numbers, fear ran rampant. The pandemic struck rapidly, and was especially violent in the close quarters of the army camps and the barracks that housed the Student Army Training Corps in Manhattan.

Sarah Hillary Battey

  • Born March 19, 1890, in Cleveland, Iowa. She graduated from Wise Memorial Hospital in Omaha in 1916.
  • She married Captain Percy B. Battey of the medical corps on September 7, 1916, and came to Manhattan when he was stationed at Camp Funston.
  • She was 28 years old when she volunteered to nurse the sick cadets of the Student Army Training Corps.

Proclamation to Close K.S.A.C.

On October 11, 1918, at the request of Governor Arthur Capper, the college announced it would close for a week. Students were asked to stay in Manhattan rather than travel home by train and spread the disease.

Closed and Reopened

  • The college reopened on November 11, 1918, the day the war ended.
  • On December 9, 1918, Governor Capper ordered it closed again. Classes resumed on December 30.

Crowds line a city street as a Liberty Loan parade passes, September 1918.

SAE House Used as Temporary Hospital

Because of the overwhelming number of patients, many students were treated in buildings rapidly converted into hospitals. The YMCA and the Sigma Alpha Epsilon and Beta Theta Pi fraternity houses all took in the sick.

Sarah Battey volunteered to help, arriving on Monday, October 14, 1918. She reported to the Sigma Alpha Epsilon fraternity house, where many of the sick cadets were being treated.

By Saturday she had fallen ill herself. She died of the pandemic flu on October 19, 1918.

From Tragedy Comes Knowledge

Millions of lives were lost in the 1918 influenza pandemic. As the average life expectancy dropped by 12 years, medical science was forced to modernize rapidly.

We learned many ways to protect ourselves from infectious disease. We learned the importance of identifying early the cause of widespread illnesses with similar symptoms, and ultimately we learned the necessity of maintaining public health.

Despite the many lives lost, a tremendous amount of knowledge was gained, and there is still much more to know.

A Town Buys an Iron Lung

In September 1937, Charles Conroy Jr., a 17-year-old from Abilene with family in Manhattan, died of infantile paralysis in a Kansas City hospital, despite being placed in an iron lung. A week later the local post of the American Legion took the first steps to buy an iron lung for Manhattan.

The Legion and the Saint Mary hospital auxiliary asked the public to pay for it. Money came from the President’s Birthday Ball fund, from women’s clubs and lodges, from junior high students, from a dance at the college, a benefit movie at the Dickinson Theater and a wolf hunt. Gifts ranged from $1 to the $400 voted by the Mrs. Abraham Lincoln Tent No. 12.

By January 21, 1938, the fund stood at $1,084.22, and the goal of about $1,250 was soon reached.

The Machine

  • Manhattan’s iron lung was built by the J. H. Emerson company of Cambridge, Massachusetts, the maker of the simplified respirator at the end of the Evolution of the Iron Lung.
  • It weighed 600 pounds and cost $1,249.50, raised by people in Manhattan and its trade territory.
  • The first model shipped was the wrong one. It was caught before it left the factory, and the right machine arrived on April 11, 1938.

A patient lies in a large tank respirator, an iron lung, with only the head outside the machine.

Ready at a Moment’s Notice

The iron lung went on display at the Community House, where townspeople came to see it. It was painted blue, and the Mercury thought it “quite attractive.” In November 1939 it moved to its permanent home at the fire station, to be used anywhere in the three-county area.

In August 1940 the health department had the police and firemen test it on three volunteers, one of them a Mercury reporter.

Weeks later it was needed. On October 11, 1940, the iron lung was rushed to Fort Riley to treat a polio patient on the reservation, and it went back again in 1941 for a little girl at Camp Whitside. The flood of 1951 damaged it beyond repair.

After the Flood

In 1953 the Legion furnished a room at the Riley County hospital, “a way of giving help to an untold number of persons in this community for years to come.”

Alfred F. Jones

Lexington, Kentucky

The earliest body-enclosing iron lung we know of was described by Alfred F. Jones of Lexington, Kentucky, in 1864. U.S. patent No. 44,198 says he “cured paralysis, neuralgia, rheumatism, seminal weakness, asthma, bronchitis, and dyspepsia. Also deafness… when properly and judiciously applied many other diseases may be cured…”

1876Plate 2 of 15

Dr. Woillez

Paris, France

In 1876 Dr. Woillez of Paris built the first workable iron lung, which he called a “spirophore.” It had the basic elements of modern respirators, including an adjustable rubber collar and a sliding bed. A unique feature was a rod which rested lightly on the patient’s chest, to give visual proof of actual lung expansion. In a brilliant lecture before the Academy of Medicine on June 20, Woillez showed a thorough understanding of the physiology and mechanics of artificial respiration. He refused to patent his invention. A colleague suggested placing spirophores all along the Seine for drowning rescues, but the money for this public service was lacking. (The illustration was reconstructed by Maxfield Parrish, Jr., for a legal battle, years before the inset photograph was discovered.)

Dr. Charles Breuillard

Paris, France

Dr. Charles Breuillard of Paris patented a “bath cabinet” type of respirator in 1887. For a source of vacuum he recommended “a steam ejector fed by a steam boiler… heated by a spirit lamp.” The patient himself was supposed to operate a valve, alternately connecting the cabinet with the vacuum for inhalation, and with the atmosphere for exhalation. Breuillard also described a chest respirator (“cuirass”) to be operated in the same way, and a face mask.

Vienna, Austria

For “resuscitating asphyxiated children,” Dr. Egon Braun of Vienna devised a small wooden box in which pressure and suction were created by the doctor’s own breath (through the tube at the right). 1901Plate 5 of 15

Rudolf Eisenmenger

Piski, Hungary

In 1901 Rudolf Eisenmenger of Piski, Hungary, patented a portable respirator which consisted of a “simple, two-part box” enclosing only the patient’s chest and abdomen. Later he became a medical professor in Vienna, and there continued to improve his invention. He stressed the importance of access to the patient’s throat and limbs, of portability, and of hand operation. (Motors were also mentioned.) There are reports of extraordinary success with Eisenmenger’s respirators.

1905Plate 6 of 15

William Davenport

London, England

William Davenport of London understood the mechanics of artificial respiration clearly. His patent in 1905 mentions a box, a rubber collar, and a simple bellows or piston pump. He lacked the sliding bed (of Woillez and modern iron lungs) but made several good suggestions, including the supplementary use of oxygen. He proposed several types, including a “collapsible form… to facilitate transit.”

Dr. Charles Morgan Hammond

Memphis, Tennessee

Dr. Charles Morgan Hammond of Memphis built his first “artificial lung” in 1905, and a series of improved models over the next 20 years. He performed experiments and treated patients successfully, but his respirators were not produced commercially and remained unknown to the public for many years.

Peter Lord

Worcester, Massachusetts

In 1908 Peter Lord of Worcester patented a respirator room. He pictured huge pistons to create the pressure changes and to supply fresh air. An enormous motor would have been needed, and very heavy construction. However, the idea of a room is good, because it makes nursing care easy.

1916Plate 9 of 15

Melvin L. Severy

Boston, Massachusetts

The patent granted to Melvin L. Severy of Boston in 1918 shows little concern for the patient’s comfort but includes ingenious mechanical elements. In the model illustrated, the patient was obliged to stand, pressing his nose and mouth against a triangular opening below two eye windows. Severy also described variable-speed pulleys and elaborate electromagnetic controls for regulating speeds and pressures. He suggested cams to produce various pressure curves. A cuirass respirator and mask resuscitator were mentioned also.

1918Plate 10 of 15

Dr. W. Steuart

South Africa

Dr. W. Steuart of South Africa built a simple respirator and demonstrated a working model to his colleagues (Medical Journal of South Africa, 1918). He proposed plaster of Paris to seal around the patient’s shoulders and pelvis, but friends quickly suggested more practical materials. Steuart was the first to think primarily of poliomyelitis. He even imagined a ward piped with intermittent suction, to which individual body casings could be attached.

Chillingworth and Hopkins

New Orleans, Louisiana

Felix P. Chillingworth and Ralph Hopkins of New Orleans built a plethysmograph to study the circulatory changes caused by lung distension. They also used it as a dog respirator. “…pulmonary ventilation is accomplished by rhythmic changes in air pressure within the plethysmograph causing alternate distention and collapse of the lungs.” (Journal of Laboratory and Clinical Medicine, 1919.)

Wilhelm Schwake

Oranienburg-Eden, Germany

Wilhelm Schwake of Oranienburg-Eden, Germany, patented a “pneumatic chamber” in 1926. He was concerned with portability and with exactly matching the patient’s depth and rhythm of breathing. He also stated that “negative pressure… upon the skin… draws out… the gaseous by-products.” We would not consider this respirator practical for severely ill patients, but the picture is a great favorite.

1928Plate 13 of 15

Philip Drinker and Louis Agassiz Shaw

Boston, Massachusetts

The first “iron lung” to receive widespread use was developed in 1928 in Boston, and was patented by Philip Drinker and Dr. Louis Agassiz Shaw. It was cumbersome and inconvenient but saved a number of lives. It had a sliding bed with a headwall attached and a rubber collar. Pressure changes were created by a rotary blower and an alternating valve. During a polio epidemic the Consolidated Gas Company of New York paid for building large numbers of these respirators, and reports of their use spread quickly around the world.

UndatedPlate 14 of 15

Wilson and Drinker

Boston, Massachusetts

In the early respirators it was difficult to give complete nursing care and to change the patients frequently. This care was found to be of life-saving importance, so Dr. James L. Wilson asked for a room in which several patients could be made to breathe at the same time. A nurse could enter by the door and perform all procedures efficiently. The room pictured was built at the Children’s Hospital in Boston and was used successfully during several epidemics.

No. 14, Emerson exhibit

1931Plate 15 of 15

John Haven Emerson

Cambridge, Massachusetts

Because of a severe poliomyelitis epidemic in 1931, John Haven Emerson of Cambridge built a simplified respirator. It cost less than half as much as others, but contained many improvements. It operated quietly, using a bellows to create the changes of pressure (as Woillez’s did, but with a motor added). A wide range of speeds was instantly available. Opening and closing were rapid and convenient. It could be pumped by hand if the electricity failed. The first Emerson “iron lung” is now preserved at the Smithsonian Institution as the prototype of the respirators built since 1931 in America and Europe. Manhattan bought one of its machines in 1938.

Communities

Polio occurred primarily in July, August, and September and hit regardless of geographic region, economic status, or population density. Relatively few people showed any symptoms and even fewer died or experienced paralysis, but the physical effects were dramatic. Communities reacted with dread because no one understood how or why people got it, and because children were the most frequently affected.

Tough Choices

During a polio epidemic, individual rights often clashed with the need for public safety. Travel and commerce between affected cities were sometimes restricted. Public health officers imposed quarantines on homes where someone was diagnosed with polio. They required the affected person to be isolated in a hospital, often against the will of the parents or family. The same practices and conflicts are seen today where SARS (Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome) breaks out.

Polio Facts

  • In 1916, New York City experienced the first large epidemic of polio, with over 9,000 cases and 2,343 deaths. The 1916 toll nationwide was 27,000 cases and 6,000 deaths. Epidemics worsened during the century. In 1952, a record 57,628 cases of polio were reported in the United States.
  • Polio (also called infantile paralysis) was most often associated with children, but it affected teens and grown-ups as well. Between 1949 and 1954, 35 percent of those who contracted polio were adults.
  • The first known polio outbreak in the United States was in Vermont in 1894. The last cases of wild (naturally occurring) polio in the United States were in 1979 in four states, among Amish residents who had refused vaccination.

A small-town main street lined with shops and parked cars, mid-twentieth century.

Families and Individuals

The enforced separation of families during the early, acute phase of the disease contributed to the intense dread and fear that polio aroused. Children and parents were not allowed any contact for ten to fourteen days and then only limited visiting for weeks afterward. When the person returned home weeks or months later, adjustment to changed circumstances brought more stress.

Tragic Stories

Polio Facts

  • Poliovirus produces no, or only minor, symptoms in 95 percent of those infected. In about 5 percent of cases, a mild form results in flu-like symptoms of fever, stiff neck, nausea, and fatigue, or a slight, temporary paralysis. About 1 percent of those with polio symptoms experience a severe form called paralytic polio that has lasting effects. In the worst cases of paralytic polio, 2 to 5 percent of children and 10 to 20 percent of adults die.
  • Humans are the only reservoir for the poliovirus. The virus does not naturally reproduce in any other species.

Children in hospital beds and wheelchairs gathered around a Christmas tree in a polio ward.

The Medical Community

Throughout most of the 20th century, hospitals operated under strict and orderly patient regimes. Nurses enforced a military-like discipline in the wards. Epidemic conditions, combined with the lack of a cure for polio, heightened everyone’s anxiety.

During a 1934 epidemic in Los Angeles, 5 percent of doctors and 11 percent of nurses who treated polio patients contracted the disease.

Disability Rights

Many people who had been injured by the poliovirus became discouraged when they returned home and encountered environmental barriers and discrimination. Some were among the most eloquent and influential leaders of the disability rights movement. Their message has been that disability is a social and civil rights issue, not simply a medical problem.

After a series of incremental policy revisions to aid people with disabilities, in 1990, the Americans with Disabilities Act was passed by the U.S. Congress. The bill became landmark civil rights legislation which creates broad legal protections for people with disabilities.

Social Effects

The effects of polio can be found throughout American culture: in the lives of people who survived it, through changes to philanthropy, and in new approaches to the design of everyday objects.

Universal Design

The seven Principles of Universal Design, published in 1997 by The Center for Universal Design at NC State University:

  1. Equitable UseThe design is useful and marketable to people with diverse abilities.
  2. Flexibility in UseThe design accommodates a wide range of individual preferences and abilities.
  3. Simple and Intuitive UseUse of the design is easy to understand, regardless of the user’s experience, knowledge, language skills, or current concentration level.
  4. Perceptible InformationThe design communicates necessary information effectively to the user, regardless of ambient conditions or the user’s sensory abilities.
  5. Tolerance for ErrorThe design minimizes hazards and the adverse consequences of accidental or unintended actions.
  6. Low Physical EffortThe design can be used efficiently and comfortably and with a minimum of fatigue.
  7. Size and Space for Approach and UseAppropriate size and space is provided for approach, reach, manipulation, and use regardless of user’s body size, posture, or mobility.

The Medical World

Polio patients were most vulnerable in the acute stage, when the virus was actively destroying the motor neurons that controlled the muscles connected to swallowing, breathing, and limb movement. Although there was, and is, no cure for polio, endangered lives could be saved. Doctors and nurses used technology, experience, and vigilance to keep patients alive until the infection ran its course, and recovery began.

Elizabeth Kenny, or Sister Kenny, as nurses were called in Australia, came to the United States in 1940. Her methods of hot-pack applications, stretching, and muscle massage were unconventional and controversial, but eventually became part of standard care for polio.

The scientific community and the government learned valuable lessons in response to polio. There were changes in government oversight of vaccine development and surveillance of clinical trials for vaccines and drugs, in design of laboratory facilities, and in the nature of rehabilitation.

Polio Facts

  • The poliovirus can destroy up to 60 percent of the motor neurons (which control muscle movement) before any symptoms of weakness or paralysis appear.
  • Before Sister Kenny brought her controversial massage, exercise, and hot-pack treatment to the United States in 1940, the accepted treatment for polio was to immobilize patients with rigid splints and casts.

The Iron Lung

Iron Lung

No device is more associated with polio than the tank respirator, better known as the iron lung. Physicians who treated people in the acute, early stage of polio saw that many patients were unable to breathe when the virus’s actions paralyzed muscle groups in the chest. Death was frequent at this stage, but those who survived usually recovered much or almost all of their former strength.

Nothing worked well in keeping people breathing until 1927, when Philip Drinker and Louis Agassiz Shaw at Harvard University devised a version of a tank respirator that could maintain respiration artificially until a person could breathe independently, usually after one or two weeks. The machine was powered by an electric motor with two vacuum cleaners. The pump changed the pressure inside a rectangular airtight metal box, pulling air in and out of the lungs.

Inventor John Emerson refined Drinker’s device and cut the cost nearly in half. Inside the tank respirator, the patient lay on a bed (sometimes called a “cookie tray”) that could slide in and out of the cylinder as needed. The side of the tank had portal windows so attendants could reach in and adjust limbs, sheets, or hot packs.

Nurses gathered around a row of iron lung respirators, a patient’s head visible at the end of one tank.

Medical Philanthropy

Three private organizations figured prominently in the history of poliomyelitis in the United States and worldwide: the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis (March of Dimes), the Rockefeller Institute, and Rotary International.

Polio Facts

  • The National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis began mass distribution of tank respirators in 1939.
  • In the 1930s, an iron lung cost about $1,500—the average price of a home. An equivalent amount in 2019 would approximate to $25,000.
  • In 1959, there were 1,200 people using tank respirators in the United States; in 2004, there were 39.

The March of Dimes

The March of Dimes

The National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis, later renamed the March of Dimes, was established in 1938 and grew out of the great success of the Birthday Balls for President Franklin Roosevelt. Its hugely successful fund-raising campaigns collected enough money to fund John Enders’s laboratory, where poliovirus was first grown in nonneural tissue; both Jonas Salk’s and Albert Sabin’s vaccine development; the 1954–55 field trial of Salk vaccine; and the supply of free vaccine to thousands of children afterward.

In 1958, the foundation changed its focus to premature birth and the prevention of birth defects. In 1979, the organization officially changed its name to the March of Dimes. Its work continues today, under the slogan “Saving babies, together.”

A young girl drops a coin into a March of Dimes collection box held by a man at a CBS radio microphone.

Rockefeller University

Rockefeller University

Industrialist John D. Rockefeller founded the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research in New York in 1901. Karl Landsteiner, who identified polio as a virus in 1908, joined the institute’s faculty in 1922, and studied human blood groups (for which he won a Nobel Prize in 1930).

Much of modern virology derives from the work of Rockefeller Institute investigators, including Simon Flexner, Thomas Rivers, and Peter Olitsky. Albert Sabin arrived in 1935 and joined them in poliomyelitis research. The institute became “Rockefeller University” in 1965 and continues to be a leading research center for the molecular biology of human diseases.

The buildings of the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research in New York, early twentieth century.

How the Poliovirus Works

Poliomyelitis is caused by a poliovirus. Viruses are particles of either RNA or DNA and are neither living nor dead. They cannot do anything until they find an appropriate cell whose internal mechanism they can commandeer to start reproducing more replicas of their viral proteins.

There are three types of poliovirus and many strains of each type. The virus enters through the mouth and multiplies in the throat and gastrointestinal tract, then moves into the bloodstream and is carried to the central nervous system where it replicates and destroys the motor neuron cells. Motor neurons control the muscles for swallowing, circulation, respiration, and the trunk, arms, and legs.

Human nerve cells have a protruding protein structure on their surface whose precise function is unknown. When poliovirus encounters the nerve cells, the protruding receptors attach to the virus particle, and infection begins. Once inside the cell, the virus hijacks the cell’s assembly process and makes thousands of copies of itself in hours. The virus kills the cell and then spreads to infect other cells.

Polio Facts

  • Many types of human cells have receptors that fit the poliovirus; no one knows why the virus favors motor neurons over other cells for replication.
  • For every 200 or so virus particles that encounter a susceptible cell, only one will successfully enter and replicate.
  • In a tissue culture, poliovirus enters cells and replicates in six to eight hours, yielding 10,000 to 100,000 virus particles per cell.
  • One way the human immune system protects itself is by producing antibodies that engage the protein covering of the poliovirus, preventing the virus from interacting with another cell.
  • There are three types of poliovirus: 1, 2 and 3. Type 1 is the most virulent and common. Both the Salk and Sabin vaccines are “trivalent”, that is, active against all three virus types. Type 2 poliovirus has not been detected anywhere in the world since 1999.
  • A person who gets polio is immune to future infection from the virus type that caused the polio.

Preventative Measures

Scientists could make vaccines even before they completely understood how they functioned. Eventually, researchers learned that vaccines work by fooling the body’s immune system into producing antibodies even though there is no disease. Jonas Salk and Albert Sabin used this knowledge to create two different kinds of polio vaccines.

A magnified field of round virus particles.

Virology

Term: Virology

Virology, the study of viruses, started in the 1890s as an offshoot of bacteriology. Viruses were defined primarily by their small size. Size was determined by the virus’s ability to pass through a filter such as this porcelain one, and by its invisibility under a light microscope. The first image of poliovirus was not captured until 1953 with an electron microscope.

Virus Facts

  • Viruses are the smallest and simplest infectious agents known so far.
  • Viruses are not cells, but have an attraction (tropism) for receptors on certain cells.
  • In addition to polio, RNA viruses include HIV, influenza A, coronaviruses, hepatitis A and C, yellow fever, rabies, mumps, and measles.

The Salk Vaccine

From the early 1900s, researchers pursued two different kinds of polio vaccine. One used inactivated (killed) viruses. The other kind used live but attenuated, or weakened, virus. The leading proponent of the killed virus was Jonas Salk.

The chief advantage of Salk’s killed virus vaccine was safety. If made properly, it could not cause disease. Its chief disadvantage was that the formaldehyde used in its manufacturing process caused the immune system to recognize killed virus differently from the live virus, possibly risking a shortened period of immunity.

Clinical Trials

Results of trials with small numbers of children in 1952 encouraged the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis to adopt Salk’s vaccine for a large-scale trial in 1954. Salk called his vaccine “Pittsburgh vaccine,” but reporters named it “Salk.”

The National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis chose Dr. Thomas Francis Jr. at the University of Michigan to implement the first mass polio vaccine trial in 1954. More than 300,000 people, mostly volunteers, including physicians, nurses, school teachers, public health officials, and community members, carried out the work.

Vaccine Facts

  • At its peak incidence in the early 1950s, poliomyelitis occurred at a rate of 13.6 cases per 100,000 population. The incidence of cancer today, by comparison is 566.1 per 100,000.
  • Edward Jenner created the first successful vaccination for a disease—smallpox—in 1796. At the time of the polio clinical trials, there were three widely used vaccines: for yellow fever (1937), rabies (1885), and smallpox. Today there are over 300 vaccines for about thirty different diseases.
  • There are two kinds of polio vaccine. IPV (Salk’s) is an injected shot used today primarily in the United States and Europe. OPV (Sabin’s) is given orally in drop form and used in global efforts to stop polio transmission.

Jonas Salk in a white lab coat holding a rack of test tubes in front of shelves of glassware.

Polio Pioneers

Polio Pioneers

In 1954, almost 75 percent of reported poliomyelitis cases occurred in people under twenty years of age, and 50 percent in children under ten. The trial’s study population, then targeted some 1.8 million children in the first three grades of elementary school at 215 test sites. In the double-blind experiment, 650,000 children received vaccine, 750,000 received a placebo (a solution made to look like vaccine, but containing no virus), and 430,000 served as controls and had neither. All were “Polio Pioneers.”

The study called for all children receiving vaccine or placebo to have three intramuscular injections over a five-week period. About 2 percent of the children also gave blood samples to verify their immune response.

Data from all 1,829,916 clinical trial participants were entered on IBM punch cards and tabulated. The study evaluated every scrap of evidence, from registration methods of the participants to laboratory procedures to statistical analysis.

Jonas Salk holding up a test tube and examining it, racks of glassware behind him.

The Sabin Vaccine

While the large-scale clinical trial with Salk vaccine went ahead in 1954, Albert Sabin pursued development of a live-virus vaccine. Like many researchers of the day, Sabin strongly disagreed with Salk’s approach of using injected, “killed” virus. He believed that long-term immunity could only be achieved with a live, attenuated—or weakened—virus.

Sabin and other researchers had argued that long-term immunity to polio could only be achieved with a live though greatly weakened virus, and that it must follow the same route of infection, through intestinal tissue. Weakening the virus required passing it through a succession of animals: rats, mice, or monkeys. This allowed it to become more virulent for these hosts, and less so for humans.

An important feature of Sabin’s oral polio vaccine was that immediately after vaccination, people shed weakened virus in their fecal waste. This boosted immunity for others in the community and gradually reduced the number of people susceptible to poliomyelitis.

Unfortunate Accidents

In the race to develop a safe and effective polio vaccine, accidents occurred with both types. For instance in 1955, the vaccine produced by Cutter Laboratories in Berkeley, California, insufficiently killed virus infected some 200 children; many were paralyzed and several died. But the global end to polio transmission would have been inconceivable without both the “killed” (Salk) and “live” (Sabin) vaccines. Neither Jonas Salk nor Albert Sabin patented their vaccines; they donated the rights as gifts to humanity.

Competing Ideas

Between 1963 and 1999, Sabin’s live vaccine largely replaced Salk’s killed vaccine everywhere in the world. However, because the live virus in the vaccine occasionally became strong enough to cause actual disease, Salk’s killed-type vaccine has replaced the live type in the United States.

Introduction

Vaccines have greatly reduced the prevalence of disease, and they continue to be important for global health today. This part of the exhibit explains how vaccines work, which diseases they prevent, and who in the world is protected by them.

How Vaccines Work

A vaccine trains the immune system to recognize a germ before the real infection ever arrives.

Because the body has already made antibodies and memory cells, it can fight off the real virus or bacteria quickly, often before a person ever feels sick.

Polio Vaccines

  • Jonas Salk’s injected vaccine (IPV, 1955) uses poliovirus that has been killed, so it cannot cause infection.
  • Albert Sabin’s oral vaccine (OPV, licensed in 1961) uses a weakened live virus. It is given as drops or on a sugar cube, and it also stops the virus multiplying in the gut.

A row of small vaccine vials with blue labels, out of focus.

The Origin of Vaccination

Vaccination has a long history. An early form was called variolation, or more broadly inoculation. Practised for centuries in Asia, it was a technique of deliberate smallpox infection: dried smallpox scabs were blown up the nose to give the person a form of the disease that was often milder.

By the 1700s variolation had spread to Africa, India and the Ottoman Empire, and then to Britain and America, where the infection was more often given through a puncture in the skin.

Variolation did work, but the risks were large. People who were variolated could catch the severe form of smallpox and die, and they could also pass the disease on to others.

A painting of a Chinese physician blowing powdered smallpox scabs into a child’s nose through a long tube.

A Scientific Method

In 1796 the English physician Edward Jenner demonstrated another method of inoculation, using cowpox. Cowpox is a disease similar to smallpox, and it had been observed that a cowpox infection could protect against smallpox.

Jenner took matter from a cowpox sore and inoculated his gardener’s eight-year-old son, James Phipps. Two months later he exposed the boy to smallpox. When Phipps did not develop smallpox, Jenner concluded that he was protected against the disease.

Jenner called the procedure vaccination, from vacca, the Latin word for cow, because the first vaccine came from the cowpox virus.

A painting of Edward Jenner vaccinating a young boy as a woman holds the child.

Efforts of Eradication

By the middle of the 20th century, confidence grew that smallpox could be the first disease humankind would eradicate. In 1967 the World Health Organization launched a global smallpox eradication program. Mass vaccination of over 80 percent of each country’s population followed, but people who were nomadic or lived in politically unstable regions posed particular problems.

To reach large populations, the epidemiologist William Foege developed Eradication Escalation, which concentrated on containing outbreaks in the season when smallpox spread least, when stopping just a few cases could break a chain of transmission.

Other measures included forcibly vaccinating an Indian religious leader to convince his followers to be vaccinated, negotiating ceasefires to move vaccines through war-torn Nigeria, vaccinating in rings around each outbreak, and paying cash rewards for reporting smallpox cases. The last naturally contracted case of smallpox was reported in Somalia in 1977, and in 1980 the WHO declared smallpox eradicated.

What Makes a Disease Eradicable?

A disease can only be eradicated if it is infectious, caught from other people or animals. Non-infectious diseases such as heart disease or cancer cannot be eradicated.

We also need measures that stop it spreading. Those measures grow with our scientific understanding and technology, and this is where human ingenuity makes the fight possible. Smallpox is gone; polio is close.

Three men in shirts and ties reading a magazine together beneath the seal of the U.S. Public Health Service.

Vaccine Innovations

Finding the germ behind a disease is only the first step. For some diseases a vaccine followed within ten years; for others the wait has lasted more than a century.

Polio’s Wait

The poliovirus was identified in 1908. It took 47 years, two world wars and the March of Dimes before the Salk vaccine was licensed in 1955.

A colorized electron micrograph of virus particles.

What Vaccines Did

Before vaccines, these diseases were part of ordinary American life. Comparing the years just before each vaccine with the years after shows how completely they changed it.

Around the World

  • The World Health Organization estimates that 2 to 3 million deaths are prevented every year by vaccines against diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough and measles.
  • Vaccine-preventable diseases still take about 1.5 million lives a year, and 19.5 million infants miss out on basic vaccines.
  • About 85 percent of children receive the first dose of a vaccine, but only 64 percent receive the second, leaving many under-vaccinated.

A drawing of a crowd of people in blue, with one person in red.

Diphtheria, Tetanus and Pertussis

Diphtheria, Tetanus and Pertussis

Doctors usually fight diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis with one combination vaccine (DTP), given in a single dose against all three bacterial diseases.

Diphtheria infects the throat and upper airways and is fatal in 5 to 10 percent of cases. Tetanus is not passed from person to person: its bacteria live in soil and animal intestines, enter through wounds, and release a toxin that causes muscle stiffness and spasms.

Pertussis, or whooping cough, is a highly contagious disease of the airways. Children who catch it have coughing spells that last four to eight weeks, and it is deadliest in young infants. Vaccinating health workers and pregnant women is the best way to protect babies too young to be vaccinated.

A colorized electron micrograph of rod-shaped bacteria.

Haemophilus influenzae type b

Haemophilus influenzae Type b

Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) is a bacterial infection that causes meningitis and pneumonia. It spreads through the airways from infected people to those who are not immune.

Despite its name, it is not the cause of influenza. It was first found in patients during the 1890 flu pandemic and was mistakenly blamed for the disease.

A colorized electron micrograph of clustered bacteria.

Hepatitis B

Hepatitis B

Hepatitis B is a highly contagious viral infection that attacks the liver. It spreads through contact with the blood or other body fluids of an infected person.

About 100,000 people die each year of chronic liver disease caused by hepatitis B. The World Health Organization recommends that every infant receive a first dose of vaccine as soon as possible after birth, preferably within 24 hours.

A colorized electron micrograph of a virus particle.

Poliomyelitis

Poliomyelitis

Poliomyelitis, often known as polio, is a highly infectious viral disease. Once the poliovirus invades the nervous system it can cause irreversible paralysis in a matter of hours. There is no cure, only treatment to ease the symptoms.

In the 1980s there were 350,000 cases of paralytic polio in the world every year. By 2016 the number had fallen to 42.

A colorized electron micrograph of virus particles.

Measles, Mumps and Rubella

Measles, Mumps and Rubella

Measles, mumps and rubella are all viral diseases, prevented together by the MMR vaccine.

Measles is a highly contagious killer of young children, despite a safe and effective vaccine. Around 90,000 people die of measles every year, 87 percent of them children under five. Vaccination cut measles deaths by 84 percent between 2000 and 2016.

Mumps spreads by direct contact or airborne droplets and causes painful swelling under the ears, fever and aches. Rubella is usually mild in children, but infection early in pregnancy causes complications in up to 90 percent of cases.

A colorized electron micrograph of round virus particles.

Meningococcal Meningitis

Meningococcal Meningitis

Meningococcal meningitis is a bacterial disease that can cause severe brain damage and is often deadly. It spreads through respiratory droplets and secretions, and kills more than 120,000 people every year.

A colorized electron micrograph of round bacteria.

Tuberculosis

Tuberculosis

Tuberculosis is a bacterial disease spread by people with tuberculosis of the lungs. The BCG vaccine (Bacille Calmette-Guérin) is only partly effective: it gives children some protection against forms such as tuberculous meningitis, but is unreliable against tuberculosis of the lungs in adults.

Every year, 1.2 million people die of tuberculosis.

A colorized electron micrograph of rod-shaped bacteria.

Yellow Fever

Yellow Fever

Yellow fever is a viral disease spread by infected mosquitoes. Forty-two countries and territories in Africa and the Americas are at risk, and in those places vaccine coverage is estimated at only 45 percent.

A colorized electron micrograph of a virus particle.

Herd Immunity

There is a shared benefit when more people are vaccinated. For most diseases, the greater the share of people who are immune, the better protected everyone is, because the disease can no longer spread easily. This community protection is called herd immunity.

Herd immunity especially protects people who cannot be vaccinated, such as babies who are too young, or children whose immune systems are weakened. They are the first victims when vaccination rates fall.

The more contagious a disease, the higher the share of people who must be immune to stop it. Measles and whooping cough spread so easily that more than nine in ten people need to be protected. Two doses of measles vaccine give 99 percent protection; without vaccination, the lifetime risk of catching measles is nearly 100 percent.
